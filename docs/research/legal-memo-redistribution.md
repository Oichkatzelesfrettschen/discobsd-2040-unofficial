## 1. Questions presented and short answers

**Question 1.** Does the tree's `NOTICE` file, together with the on-device `/etc/COPYRIGHT`, satisfy the attribution and notice conditions of every license in the DiscoBSD/rp2040 image for each of the two contemplated distribution channels?

**Short answer.** For the GitHub release channel, substantially yes, with one gap and one weakness. `NOTICE` reproduces the required copyright notices, condition lists, and disclaimers, which is what the "documentation and/or other materials provided with the distribution" clauses demand. The gap is Apache License 2.0 section 4(a), which requires that recipients of the CoreMark object code be given "a copy of this License"; `NOTICE` section 13 supplies a URL to apache.org rather than the text. The weakness is that a release page satisfies these clauses only if `NOTICE` is actually attached as a release asset and its text appears in the release body -- a bare hyperlink is not reproduction.

For the pre-flashed board channel, no, not as presently contemplated. A board sold with a listing and no printed material relies entirely on `/etc/COPYRIGHT`, which is a short form that omits the condition lists and the warranty disclaimers that every BSD-, ISC-, MIT-, NCSA-, and Caldera-class license requires a binary redistributor to reproduce, and which is unreadable to a buyer whose board is dead on arrival. The full `NOTICE` text must appear on the listing page itself, and a printed card should accompany the board. See Section 3.

**Question 2.** Is the Caldera International ancient-UNIX license of January 23, 2002 a valid and enforceable grant covering the AT&T-descended code in 2.11BSD and the Sixth Edition pack, and what residual risk does the client assume by relying on it?

**Short answer.** The grant is a well-formed copyright license whose conditions are enforceable as conditions under *Jacobsen v. Katzer*, 535 F.3d 1373 (Fed. Cir. 2008). Its vulnerability is title, not form: the Tenth Circuit's decision in *SCO Group, Inc. v. Novell, Inc.*, 578 F.3d 1313 (10th Cir. 2009), and the 2010 jury verdict and ensuing judgment on remand established that Novell, not Caldera's successor, retained the UNIX and UnixWare copyrights under the 1995 Asset Purchase Agreement. Caldera International therefore granted in 2002 what a jury later found it did not own. The practical risk is nonetheless **low** -- estoppel and implied license arising from more than two decades of open distribution by the Ancient UNIX Archive with the successive title-holders' knowledge, the absence of any enforcement action in the history of the field, the chain of indifferent owners (Novell, Attachmate in 2011, Micro Focus, now OpenText), and damages that would be trivial at hobbyist volume. The client should not treat the risk as zero and should not represent to buyers that the matter is settled. See Section 4 for the rating and the required acknowledgement wording.

**Question 3.** May the product ship a program named `coremark` and may the listing say that the board runs CoreMark?

A number produced that way is not a canonical report, and publishing it as a bare CoreMark 1.0 score is the real exposure. Either omit the score from the listing or label it as a non-certified integer-mode run and disclose compiler version, flags, and iteration count. See Section 5.

**Question 4.** What trademark constraints govern the listing's references to Raspberry Pi, Pico, UNIX, DiscoBSD, RetroBSD, and BSD?

**Short answer.** All permitted uses are nominative. The board is a genuine Raspberry Pi Pico, so its resale is a resale of a genuine article and the marks may be used to identify it; the product name must not contain "Raspberry Pi" or "Pico," no logo may appear except as it appears on the genuine goods, and a no-endorsement disclaimer should be conspicuous. *New Kids on the Block v. **Question 5.** What warranty and consumer-protection law governs the sale of a pre-flashed board from or into California?

**Short answer.** The open-source warranty disclaimers do not reach the buyer. ## 3. Analysis A: `NOTICE`, `/etc/COPYRIGHT`, and the README's six redistributor steps

### 3.1 The governing question for every license class

Twelve of the fourteen components in the image impose a variant of one condition on binary redistribution: reproduce the copyright notice, the list of conditions, and the warranty disclaimer "in the documentation and/or other materials provided with the distribution." The phrase is boilerplate inherited from the 1988 Berkeley license and repeated, with minor variation, in every BSD-, ISC-, MIT-, NCSA-, and Caldera-style text in the tree.

Two interpretive questions decide the whole of Analysis A.

**First: what counts as "materials provided with the distribution"?** The clause is a condition on a copyright license, and *Jacobsen v. Katzer*, 535 F.3d 1373, 1381-83 (Fed. Cir. 2008), holds that such conditions limit the scope of the grant, so that distributing outside them is infringement rather than mere breach of contract. Failure to reproduce is therefore not a trivial default; it is a use outside the license, and the remedy set includes injunctive relief with a presumption of irreparable harm. *Artifex Software, Inc. v. Hancom, Inc.*, No. 16-cv-06982-JSC, 2017 WL 1477373 (N.D. Cal. Apr. 25, 2017), reinforces the point from the contract side, holding that an open-source license may also be enforced as a contract and that the absence of signature or payment does not defeat formation where the licensee accepted the benefit.

The clause's plain sense is that the material travels with the copy. Something the recipient receives at the time of and as part of the distribution qualifies. Something the recipient must go elsewhere to obtain does not, however easy the trip.

**Second: does a hyperlink satisfy the clause?** No, and the client should not rely on the contrary view.

A hyperlink is a pointer, not a reproduction. The verb in every one of these clauses is "reproduce" or "retain," and neither is accomplished by naming a location. The content behind a link is not "provided with the distribution" in any ordinary sense: it is provided by a different server, at a later time, on terms the distributor does not control, and it may change or vanish. A recipient who downloads a UF2 file and never follows the link has received a copy of the work without the notice the license conditions the copy on.

The counterargument -- that in a networked distribution, a link is how documentation is conventionally provided, and that a recipient who obtained the binary from a web page has necessarily seen that page -- has some force for the release channel, where the download and the page are the same transaction. It has none for a physical board, where the buyer holds the goods and the listing page is nowhere in the package. The failure mode is concrete rather than hypothetical: a buyer whose board will not boot cannot read `/etc/COPYRIGHT` either, and is left with a copy of the software and no notice at all.

The prudent rule, and the one recommended here, is that the notice text itself must be present in the channel, and a link may supplement but never substitute. This costs the client nothing: `NOTICE` is 311 lines of plain text.

### 3.2 License class by license class

Each subsection states the operative condition, then assesses (i) UF2 files on a release page and (ii) a physical board with a listing and no printed material.

#### 3.2.1 BSD 3-Clause: DiscoBSD and RetroBSD

**Operative condition.** Binary redistribution must reproduce the copyright notice, the three-clause condition list, and the all-caps disclaimer in the documentation or other materials provided with the distribution. The third clause forbids use of the copyright holder's or contributors' names to endorse or promote products derived from the software without prior written permission.

**(i) Release page.** Satisfied where `NOTICE` is attached as a release asset and its section 1 text appears in the release body. `NOTICE` section 1 reproduces all three elements verbatim.

**(ii) Board.** Not satisfied by `/etc/COPYRIGHT` alone. The short form names DiscoBSD and RetroBSD as holders but reproduces neither the condition list nor the disclaimer. The cure is the full text on the listing page and on a shipped card.

**Note on the `LICENSE` file.** The repository's `LICENSE` retains the template placeholder `{organization}` in its third clause, where `NOTICE` correctly reads "the copyright holder." The placeholder does not void the clause -- the copyright holder is identified in the first two lines of the same file, and a court construing the document as a whole would read the clause as naming DiscoBSD -- but it is an obvious defect in a document the client will point buyers at, and it should be corrected. Marked prudent in the Section 10 checklist rather than required, because no obligation of the client's turns on it.

#### 3.2.2 The Berkeley notices of 1980-1993, and the 1999 withdrawal

**Operative condition.** Two forms appear in the tree. The older kernel files carry a bare reference: "Copyright (c) 1980, 1986 Regents of the University of California. All rights reserved. The Berkeley software License Agreement specifies the terms and conditions for redistribution." The libc and later utilities carry the 1988 form, which requires that the notice and paragraph be duplicated in all forms, that documentation and other distribution materials acknowledge development by the University of California, Berkeley, and that the University's name not be used for endorsement.

The advertising acknowledgement was withdrawn by the University's letter of July 22, 1999 (`README.Impt.License.Change`, Office of Technology Licensing, William Hoskins): "Effective immediately, licensees and distributors are no longer required to include the acknowledgement within advertising materials." The withdrawal is precise in its scope. It removes the obligation to place the acknowledgement in *advertising*. It does not remove the obligation to reproduce the notice and its paragraph in the documentation and materials accompanying a binary distribution, which remains live.

**(i) Release page.** Satisfied by `NOTICE` section 2, which reproduces both notice forms and quotes the withdrawal letter.

**(ii) Board.** Not satisfied by `/etc/COPYRIGHT` alone, for the same reason: the short form carries the Berkeley acknowledgement sentence but not the condition paragraph or the disclaimer. Note that the acknowledgement sentence in `/etc/COPYRIGHT` is now a courtesy, not a requirement, and the client should understand it as such so as not to over-comply in one place while under-complying in another.

**A caution on the older form.** The 1980/1986 notice incorporates by reference a "Berkeley software License Agreement" that is not reproduced in the tree and whose terms are not in `NOTICE`. In practice the entire 2.11BSD corpus is distributed by the Ancient UNIX Archive and its mirrors on the understanding that the Berkeley terms are the permissive ones reproduced in the 1988 form, and no contrary reading has ever been asserted. The client inherits that understanding. It is listed in Section 11 as an item for attorney confirmation.

#### 3.2.3 The Caldera license of 2002

**Operative condition.** Four conditions. Source redistributions must retain the notice, conditions, and disclaimer. Binary redistributions must reproduce them in the documentation or other materials provided with the distribution. "All advertising materials mentioning features or use of this software must display the following acknowledgement: This product includes software developed or owned by Caldera International, Inc." And the Caldera name may not be used for endorsement without written permission.

The advertising clause is the distinguishing feature. Unlike the Berkeley advertising clause, it has never been withdrawn. No successor in title has issued anything comparable to the University's 1999 letter. It is live.

**(i) Release page.** The notice condition is satisfied by `NOTICE` section 3. The advertising condition is engaged if the release page describes features -- and a release page that lists the shell, the editors, and the V6 emulator does describe features. Include the acknowledgement sentence in the release body. The cost is one sentence and the argument against it is nonexistent.

**(ii) Board.** The notice condition is not satisfied by `/etc/COPYRIGHT` alone; the same cure applies. The advertising condition is squarely engaged by a Tindie listing that names `sh`, `ed`, `sed`, `awk`, `find`, or the Sixth Edition pack. The acknowledgement sentence is mandatory in that listing.

Section 4 addresses the enforceability of this license and supplies the exact wording and placement.

#### 3.2.4 Raspberry Pi boot2, BSD 3-Clause

**Operative condition.** Identical in substance to 3.2.1: reproduce notice, conditions, and disclaimer in binary distribution materials; no endorsement use of the holder's name.

**(i) Release page.** Satisfied by `NOTICE` section 4.

**(ii) Board.** Not satisfied by the short form; same cure. Note the distinct trademark issue: `NOTICE` section 4 already carries the sentence "Raspberry Pi and Pico are trademarks of Raspberry Pi Ltd. This project is not endorsed by Raspberry Pi Ltd.; the firmware runs on a Raspberry Pi Pico." That sentence is good practice and should be echoed on the listing, but it is a trademark disclaimer, not a copyright-license condition, and is analyzed in Section 6.

#### 3.2.5 ISC: Dhara, heatshrink, and the DiscoBSD port drivers

**Operative condition.** "Permission to use, copy, modify, and/or distribute this software for any purpose with or without fee is hereby granted, provided that the above copyright notice and this permission notice appear in all copies." The condition attaches to *copies*, not to documentation, which is a subtly broader formulation: the notice must appear in all copies, and a UF2 image is a copy.

**(i) Release page.** `NOTICE` sections 5 and 6, and the ISC paragraph in section 1, reproduce the notices, and shipping `NOTICE` with the release assets places them alongside every copy. A strict reading -- that the notice must be *inside* the copy -- is not how the ISC license has ever been applied to compiled binaries, where embedding the text would be absurd, and accompanying materials are universally accepted. The client is compliant.

**(ii) Board.** Here the analysis is marginally better than for the BSD classes, because the copy on the board is accompanied by `/etc/COPYRIGHT` on the same medium. But `/etc/COPYRIGHT` does not reproduce the ISC permission notice or its disclaimer; it names Dhara and heatshrink's authors only. Same cure: full text on the listing and the card.

#### 3.2.6 NCSA / MIT: compiler_rt soft-float routines

**Operative condition.** The University of Illinois/NCSA Open Source License requires that binary redistributions reproduce the copyright notice, the condition list, and the disclaimers "in the documentation and/or other materials provided with the distribution," and forbids use of the names of the LLVM Team, the University of Illinois at Urbana-Champaign, or contributors for endorsement without written permission. The alternative MIT grant requires the notice and permission notice in all copies or substantial portions.

**(i) Release page.** Satisfied by `NOTICE` section 7.

**(ii) Board.** Not satisfied by the short form -- `/etc/COPYRIGHT` names "compiler_rt" among the authors but reproduces nothing. Same cure.

One point deserves attention. `NOTICE` section 7 attributes the copyright to "the contributors listed in `lib/libc/runtime/CREDITS.txt`" rather than reproducing the list. The NCSA license requires reproduction of "the above copyright notice," and where the notice itself incorporates a credits file by reference, reproducing the reference is a fair reading of the obligation. It would nonetheless be safer to reproduce `CREDITS.txt` in the release assets. Prudent, not required.

#### 3.2.7 BSD 2-Clause: Smaller C

**Operative condition.** Two clauses only: retain in source, reproduce in binary distribution materials. There is no endorsement clause.

**(i) Release page.** Satisfied by `NOTICE` section 8.

**(ii) Board.** Not satisfied by the short form; same cure.

#### 3.2.8 The Vakulenko MIT/X-style notice, with its no-advertising clause

**Operative condition.** This is the one license in the tree whose advertising restriction is a *prohibition* rather than a requirement, and it is easy to trip over. The grant is conditioned on three things: the copyright notice appearing in all copies; the copyright notice, the permission notice, and the warranty disclaimer appearing in supporting documentation; and -- the operative clause -- "that the name of the author not be used in advertising or publicity pertaining to distribution of the software without specific, written prior permission."

**(i) Release page.** The notice conditions are satisfied by `NOTICE` section 9. The no-advertising clause is satisfied so long as the release page does not use Serge Vakulenko's name in its promotional text. Reproducing the copyright notice in `NOTICE` is not "use in advertising or publicity"; it is compliance with the notice condition, and reading the two clauses to conflict would make the license impossible to satisfy. The client should nonetheless keep the author's name out of the release's descriptive prose.

**(ii) Board.** Same. The Tindie listing must not say anything like "includes Serge Vakulenko's assembler." It may say "includes an assembler and linker." Attribution belongs in `NOTICE`; it does not belong in the sales copy. This is the inverse of the Caldera rule, and the client should hold both in mind at once: the Caldera sentence is *required* in the listing and the Vakulenko name is *forbidden* in it.

#### 3.2.9 sbase, MIT

**Operative condition.** "The above copyright notice and this permission notice shall be included in all copies or substantial portions of the Software."

**(i) Release page.** Satisfied by `NOTICE` section 10, which reproduces the three principal copyright lines and incorporates the contributor list at `usr.bin/textbox/LICENSE` by reference.

**(ii) Board.** Not satisfied by the short form; same cure. As with NCSA, reproducing the full contributor list rather than a reference would be safer. Prudent.

#### 3.2.10 stevie, the Unlicense

**Operative condition.** None. The Unlicense is a public-domain dedication with a fallback license granting unrestricted use, and it imposes no attribution requirement on any distributor.

**(i) and (ii).** Both satisfied with nothing. `NOTICE` section 11 records the status as a courtesy and for accuracy of the inventory. Nothing must change.

#### 3.2.11 The PDP-11 emulator, WTFPL version 2

**Operative condition.** None that binds a distributor. The WTFPL's sole substantive term permits copying, modification, and distribution without condition; its one restriction is directed at derivative *licenses* rather than at software. No attribution is required.

**(i) and (ii).** Both satisfied. `NOTICE` section 12 is courtesy. Nothing must change.

One practical remark unrelated to obligation: the license's name is vulgar, and it is reproduced verbatim in `NOTICE` section 12. The client may prefer not to surface that text on a consumer listing. Omitting the license's name from the listing while retaining it in `NOTICE` breaches nothing, since no condition requires its display.

#### 3.2.12 Apache License 2.0, section 4: CoreMark

**Operative condition.** Section 4 imposes four conditions on redistribution in any medium, with or without modification, in source or object form.

Section 4(a): "You must give any other recipients of the Work or Derivative Works a copy of this License."

Section 4(b): modified files must carry prominent notices stating that they were changed.

Section 4(c): source-form derivative works must retain all copyright, patent, trademark, and attribution notices.

Section 4(d): if the Work includes a NOTICE text file as part of its distribution, derivative works must include a readable copy of its attribution notices.

**Assessment of each.** Section 4(d) is not triggered: the directory listing of `benchmarks/coremark` contains no `NOTICE` file, so the upstream Work does not include one and there is nothing to reproduce. Section 4(b) is not triggered as to the protected sources, which are unmodified; it is arguably triggered as to `core_portme.c` and `core_portme.h`, which are upstream template files the client has filled in. Those files do carry extensive comments describing exactly what the port changed and why, which satisfies the "prominent notices stating that You changed the files" requirement on any reasonable reading. Section 4(c) binds only source-form distribution of derivative works; the client distributes object form in both channels, and distributes source through the public repository, where the notices are intact.

**Section 4(a) is the live gap.** The client distributes the compiled `coremark` program in object form in both channels, and section 4(a) requires that recipients be given *a copy of the License*. `NOTICE` section 13 presently states the license by name and supplies the URL `http://www.apache.org/licenses/LICENSE-2.0`. A URL is not a copy. This is the one place in the entire notice apparatus where a required text is absent rather than merely abbreviated.

The cure is trivial and should be implemented before the first release or sale: ship the Apache License 2.0 text itself. The text already exists in the tree at `benchmarks/coremark/LICENSE.md`, which contains both the Acceptable Use Agreement and the full Apache text. Attach that file, or a file containing the Apache text, as a release asset alongside the UF2 files, and make it available at the URL the shipped card points to.

**(i) Release page.** Not satisfied until the Apache text accompanies the assets. Then satisfied.

**(ii) Board.** Not satisfied. `/etc/COPYRIGHT` does not contain the Apache text and neither does anything shipped with the board. Because the on-device root cannot reasonably carry a full Apache license text within its 979 KB budget, the correct cure is for the listing page and the shipped card's URL to lead to the license text, and for the release assets to carry it. If the client concludes that this is more apparatus than the benchmark is worth -- a defensible view -- the alternative is to omit `coremark` from the product image, which is a one-line change to `distrib/rp2040/mi.rp2040`. See Section 5.5.

#### 3.2.13 The GCC Runtime Library Exception, version 3.1

**Operative condition.** libgcc is licensed under GPL version 3 with an additional permission. Section 1 of the Exception grants permission to propagate a work of Target Code formed by combining the Runtime Library with Independent Modules, under terms of the propagator's choosing, provided that every Target Code work was generated by an Eligible Compilation Process. A Compilation Process is Eligible if it is done using GCC alone or with other GPL-compatible software, and if it does not involve making any work based on GCC other than through the normal use of its intermediate representations.

**Assessment.** The condition is satisfied on the facts. The firmware is built with `arm-none-eabi-gcc` and GNU binutils with no proprietary plugin, as the README's toolchain instructions confirm. The Exception therefore permits the client to distribute the resulting binaries under the permissive terms of the rest of the image, and no GPL obligation -- no source offer, no copyleft, no written offer under GPL section 6 -- propagates to the client or to any buyer.

**(i) and (ii).** Both satisfied. No notice obligation attaches to the Exception itself; `NOTICE` section 14 records the analysis for the reader's benefit, which is good practice and not required. The client should preserve the build's use of an unmodified GCC toolchain, because the Exception's protection depends on it: if a future build introduced a proprietary compiler plugin, the compilation process would cease to be Eligible and libgcc's GPL terms would reach the binaries.

### 3.3 Summary of what must change

Consolidating the above, three changes are required before either channel opens, and three more are prudent.

**Required.** First, the full text of `NOTICE` must appear on the Tindie listing page itself, not merely be linked from it. Second, the Apache License 2.0 text must accompany the CoreMark binary in both channels, or `coremark` must be omitted from the product image; these are not neutral alternatives, and Section 5.5 recommends omission from the product image as the preferred course. Third, `NOTICE` must be attached as a release asset and its text placed in the release body, rather than merely referenced.

**Prudent.** A printed card in the box carrying the copyright lines and a URL; reproduction of the `CREDITS.txt` and sbase contributor lists rather than references to them; correction of the `{organization}` placeholder in `LICENSE`.

### 3.4 The README's six redistributor steps, assessed

## 4. Analysis B: the Caldera license

### 4.1 The instrument

The Caldera International ancient-UNIX license is a letter dated January 23, 2002, signed by Bill Broderick, Director of Licensing Services, Caldera International, Inc., distributed as `AncientUnix.pdf` alongside the V6 and V7 disk images in `uv6swre.zip` and `uv7swre.zip`. Its full text is reproduced in the port tree at `usr.bin/look/LICENSE` and in the PDF at `usr.bin/pdp11/Caldera-license.pdf`.

The operative granting language is: "Caldera International, Inc. hereby grants a fee free license that includes the rights use, modify and distribute this named source code, including creating derived binary products created from the source code." The subject matter is "32-bit 32V UNIX" and "16 bit UNIX Versions 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7," with express exclusion of System III, System V, and their successors. The grant is then subjected to a BSD-style four-condition regime: retain in source, reproduce in binary distribution materials, display the acknowledgement in all advertising materials mentioning features or use, and no endorsement use of the Caldera name.

Two features of the instrument matter. It is unilateral -- a grant, not a negotiated agreement -- and it is unlimited in time and unconditional as to revocation. Nothing in the letter reserves a right to revoke, and its terms are perpetual on their face.

### 4.2 Enforceability as a copyright license condition

Assuming for the moment that the grantor held title, the instrument is enforceable and the client's compliance obligations are real.

*Jacobsen v. Katzer*, 535 F.3d 1373 (Fed. Cir. 2008), is the controlling framework. The Federal Circuit held that the attribution and notice terms of the Artistic License were conditions limiting the scope of the license, not independent covenants, so that use outside them was copyright infringement rather than breach of contract. The court reasoned, at 1381-82, that copyright holders who grant nonexclusive licenses for no money "have the right to control the modification and distribution of copyrighted material" and that the conditions serve real economic purposes -- attribution, provenance, and the tracking of downstream use -- notwithstanding the absence of a royalty. The consequence at 1381 is significant: because the terms are conditions, the licensor may seek injunctive relief under the Copyright Act with its attendant presumptions, rather than being limited to contract damages measured by a zero price.

The Caldera conditions are structurally identical to the Artistic License conditions *Jacobsen* addressed. They are introduced by "provided that the following conditions are met," which is the classic language of condition. A court would hold that distributing 2.11BSD's AT&T-descended binaries without reproducing the notice, or advertising their features without the acknowledgement sentence, exceeds the scope of the grant.

*Artifex Software, Inc. v. Hancom, Inc.*, 2017 WL 1477373 (N.D. Cal. Apr. 25, 2017), supplies the parallel contract theory. The court denied summary judgment to a defendant who argued that no contract formed because it never signed the GNU GPL and paid nothing, holding that the plaintiff had adequately alleged formation through the defendant's use of the software on the license's stated terms, and that the dual-licensing business model supplied consideration. Applied here, a court could find that the client accepted the Caldera terms by exercising the grant, giving the successor in title a contract claim in addition to, or in the alternative to, a copyright claim.

The practical takeaway is that the acknowledgement sentence is not optional politeness. Section 4.6 specifies where it goes.

### 4.3 Who owned the UNIX copyrights in 2002

The grantor's title is the vulnerability.

The chain runs as follows. AT&T developed UNIX and held the copyrights. Novell acquired the UNIX business from AT&T's Unix System Laboratories in 1993. In 1995, Novell and The Santa Cruz Operation executed an Asset Purchase Agreement transferring the UNIX business to SCO. Schedule 1.1(b) of that agreement, the schedule of excluded assets, listed "All copyrights and trademarks, except for the trademarks UNIX and UnixWare." In 2001, Caldera Systems acquired SCO's UNIX division and the resulting entity was renamed Caldera International, Inc. In 2002 -- in the same year as the ancient-UNIX letter -- Caldera International renamed itself The SCO Group.

The entity that issued the January 2002 letter is therefore the entity that became The SCO Group, and the question of what it owned is precisely the question litigated for the following decade.

In *SCO Group, Inc. v. Novell, Inc.*, 578 F.3d 1313 (10th Cir. 2009), the Tenth Circuit reversed the district court's grant of summary judgment that had held Novell retained the copyrights, finding the Asset Purchase Agreement and its amendments ambiguous and the extrinsic evidence conflicting, such that the question of copyright ownership was for a jury. The case was remanded for trial. On March 30, 2010, a jury in the District of Utah returned a verdict finding that the 1995 agreement did not transfer the UNIX and UnixWare copyrights to Santa Cruz Operation. The district court entered judgment on that verdict and denied SCO's post-trial motions, and the Tenth Circuit affirmed. The determination is that **Novell, not Caldera or its successor, held the UNIX copyrights in 2002.**

The conclusion follows: when Caldera International signed the ancient-UNIX letter in January 2002, it purported to grant rights in copyrights that a jury later found it did not own. Under ordinary principles, a licensor cannot convey what it does not hold. On the face of the record, the 2002 grant is void as to the AT&T-derived copyrights, and every downstream distributor of V1 through V7 and 32V -- the Ancient UNIX Archive, the SIMH kit maintainers, every retrocomputing distribution, and this client -- relies on an instrument from a grantor without title.

That is the worst-case framing, and it is the honest starting point. It is not where the analysis ends.

### 4.4 Why the practical risk is nonetheless low

Six considerations, in descending order of weight.

**First, the successors in title have never asserted a claim, and their conduct is affirmatively permissive.** Novell's public posture throughout the SCO litigation was that it did not object to the open distribution of ancient UNIX, and Novell took no step at any time to disturb the Ancient UNIX Archive. Title then passed to Attachmate in Novell's 2011 acquisition, from Attachmate to Micro Focus in 2014, and from Micro Focus to OpenText in 2023. Each of those transactions moved a large enterprise-software portfolio in which pre-1980 PDP-11 source code is of no commercial consequence whatsoever. None of the four successive holders has ever sent a demand letter, filed a claim, or requested a takedown concerning ancient UNIX distribution. Twenty-four years of open, notorious, continuous distribution by an archive that exists for precisely this purpose, under an instrument the archive publishes on its own website, with no response from any title-holder, is a strong practical signal.

**Second, equitable estoppel is available and is the strongest doctrinal defense.** The elements are that the party to be estopped knew the facts, intended that its conduct be acted on or acted so that the other party had a right to believe it was so intended, the other party was ignorant of the true facts, and relied on the conduct to its injury. Each is arguable here. The title-holders knew of the distribution -- it was discussed in open court in litigation to which Novell was a party. Their silence over decades, in the face of a publicly posted license letter that the entire field visibly relied on, is conduct on which reliance was reasonable. Distributors, including this client, relied by building products and reputations on the availability of the code. The reliance is to their detriment if the rug is now pulled. Estoppel would not automatically defeat a claim, but on these facts it is a serious defense rather than a makeweight.

**Third, an implied license is arguable.** Where a copyright owner knows of and acquiesces in a use over a long period, courts have found a nonexclusive license implied from conduct. The doctrine typically requires a request, creation and delivery, and an intent to permit use, and it fits imperfectly here because the title-holders never delivered anything to these distributors. But the combination of decades of knowing acquiescence with a facially valid written grant from the entity the successors themselves treated as the operator of the UNIX business is a plausible basis for an implied license, and it would be pleaded alongside estoppel.

**Fourth, the statute of limitations helps, but less than it first appears, and the client should not be told otherwise.** Section 507(b) of the Copyright Act, 17 U.S.C. section 507(b), bars a civil action commenced more than three years after the claim accrued. Under the separate-accrual rule, each act of infringement starts its own limitations period. The consequence is precise: claims arising from distributions more than three years old are time-barred, and claims arising from *future* distributions are not. Limitations therefore caps historical exposure; it does not immunize the client's forward-looking business.

The client should also not be told that laches cures the remainder. *Petrella v. Metro-Goldwyn-Mayer, Inc.*, 572 U.S. 663 (2014), holds that laches cannot be invoked to bar a claim for damages brought within the section 507(b) window, reasoning that Congress made the timeliness judgment in the statute and that courts may not override it. *Petrella* leaves a narrow role for laches in limiting equitable relief in extraordinary circumstances, and it preserves estoppel as a distinct defense, at 684-85. The "twenty years of silence" argument therefore does its work through estoppel, not laches, and this memorandum frames it that way.

**Fifth, damages would be minimal and statutory damages are likely unavailable.** Actual damages for the unauthorized distribution of forty-year-old PDP-11 utilities embedded in a hobbyist board sold in small quantities approach zero, and the infringer's profits attributable to the infringing components would be a small fraction of a small margin. Statutory damages and attorney's fees under 17 U.S.C. sections 504(c) and 505 are available only for works registered before the infringement or within three months of first publication, 17 U.S.C. section 412; registration status for these works is unknown and is listed in Section 11. Without statutory damages, the economics of a claim are unattractive to any plaintiff.

**Sixth, the client adds no incremental risk.** The exposure is the field's, not this project's. Every distributor of 2.11BSD, V6, and V7 binaries carries it identically. A claim against this client would be a claim against the entire retrocomputing ecosystem, brought by a party that has shown no interest in twenty-four years.

### 4.5 Risk rating

**Low.** Specifically: the probability of an enforcement action by OpenText or any predecessor in title against a hobbyist-scale seller of pre-flashed boards is very low; the probability that such an action, if brought, would succeed against a properly pleaded estoppel and implied-license defense is moderate at best for the claimant; and the quantum of exposure, absent statutory damages, is small. The client should proceed.

Three caveats qualify the rating. It is a rating of *practical* risk, not a legal opinion that the 2002 grant is valid -- on the face of the SCO v. Novell record it likely is not, as to the AT&T copyrights. It assumes the client remains at hobbyist scale; a commercial venture of consequence, or one that attracted attention by marketing the UNIX heritage aggressively, would shift the calculus. And it assumes full compliance with the Caldera conditions, because a distributor who complied would be in a materially better equitable position than one who did not -- compliance is itself part of why the risk is low.

The client should make no representation to buyers that the ancient-UNIX licensing question is settled. The listing should not say "fully licensed" or "all rights cleared." Saying so would be inaccurate, and inaccuracy in a listing is its own problem under Section 7.

### 4.6 Where the acknowledgement sentence must and need not appear

The sentence is exactly: **"This product includes software developed or owned by Caldera International, Inc."** It must be reproduced verbatim. It is not paraphrasable; the license specifies the text.

The clause reaches "all advertising materials mentioning features or use of this software." The boundary is therefore between *advertising* and everything else, and the client's three surfaces fall on different sides of it.

**Required: the Tindie listing,** whenever it mentions the shell, `ed`, `sed`, `awk`, `find`, `cpio`, `dd`, `look`, `deroff`, the Sixth Edition pack, or the V6 emulator, or describes the system's use in any way that draws on the AT&T-derived components. A listing that says the board "runs a shell, `vi`, `awk`, and `sed`" mentions features of the software squarely. So does one that says "boots Sixth Edition UNIX from 1975." Practically, any listing the client would actually write triggers the clause, and the client should treat the sentence as unconditionally required in the listing rather than attempting to draft around it.

**Required in substance, and in any event prudent: the GitHub release page,** where the release body describes what the firmware does. Whether a release page for free software is "advertising" is genuinely debatable -- it promotes nothing for sale -- but a release body that enumerates features is promotional in character, the cost of including one sentence is zero, and the argument is not worth having. Include it.

**Not required: the in-box card and `/etc/COPYRIGHT`.** These are documentation, not advertising. What the Caldera license requires of documentation is the *notice, condition list, and disclaimer* under the binary-redistribution clause, which is a different and larger obligation. The card must carry, or point to, the notice apparatus; it need not carry the acknowledgement sentence. That said, `/etc/COPYRIGHT` already includes the sentence, and there is no reason to remove it -- over-inclusion in documentation breaches nothing.

**Not required: the product title,** packaging exterior, or any surface that does not mention software features.

## 5. Analysis C: CoreMark

### 5.1 The two instruments and the modification determination

The CoreMark distribution carries two separate legal instruments, and conflating them is the most common error in this area.

The **software** is licensed under the Apache License, Version 2.0. That license is permissive, irrevocable by its terms (section 2 grants a "perpetual ... irrevocable copyright license"), and expressly permits modification and commercial distribution. Apache section 6 grants no trademark rights: "This License does not grant permission to use the trade names, trademarks, service marks, or product names of the Licensor, except as required for reasonable and customary use in describing the origin of the Work."

The **COREMARK word mark** is registered -- Serial No. 85/487,290, Registration No. 4,179,307 -- and is licensed separately and narrowly under the CoreMark Acceptable Use Agreement, reproduced in the tree at `benchmarks/coremark/LICENSE.md`. The AUA's preamble is explicit about the split: "Licensor offers benchmarking software (Software) pursuant to an open source license, but carefully controls use of its benchmarks and their associated goodwill."

The AUA grants, at clause 1.1, "a personal, non-exclusive, royalty-free, revocable right and license to use and display the Trademark during the term of this Agreement," and only "in connection with the Licensed Use," which the preamble defines as "the generation of data regarding measurement and analysis of computer and embedded system benchmarking via the Software." Clause 4.3 makes the rights non-assignable and non-sublicensable. Clause 4.5 selects California law, which is convenient: the client is already governed by it.

**The modification determination.** The six files that the CoreMark rules protect -- `core_main.c`, `core_list_join.c`, `core_matrix.c`, `core_state.c`, `core_util.c`, and `coremark.h` -- are unmodified. Two independent lines of evidence establish this.

The git record shows that a diff of those six files from the import commit `f5a366b1` to HEAD is empty. The only files changed in `benchmarks/coremark` since import are `Makefile`, `core_portme.c`, `core_portme.h`, and `coremark.md5`. CoreMark's own `README.md` designates `core_portme.c` and `core_portme.h` as the files a porter adjusts: "For cross compile platforms please adjust `core_portme.mak`, `core_portme.h` (and possibly `core_portme.c`) according to the specific platform used." Changes confined to those files are the vendor-contemplated porting layer, not modification of the Software.

Independent verification against upstream confirms the point: the six core files are byte-identical by MD5 to `eembc/coremark` main, and the tree's `coremark.md5` matches upstream's recorded hashes for the five `.c` files. The single divergent line, for `coremark.h`, arises because upstream's own manifest entry is stale against upstream's own committed `coremark.h`; the file contents match. The regeneration of that line in commit `09a9f496` corrected a stale hash rather than accommodating a local edit.

**Conclusion: the tree's copy of CoreMark is unmodified within the meaning of AUA clause 1.2.** The trademark license of clause 1.1 is available to the client. The client's instruction that the benchmark not be modified is satisfied by the current tree and should be maintained as a standing constraint, since the trademark license evaporates the moment a protected file is touched.

### 5.2 (i) May the product ship a program named `coremark`, and may the listing say "runs CoreMark"?

**Yes to both, subject to notices.**

The program may be named `coremark` and installed at `/usr/bin/coremark`. This is use of the mark on an unmodified copy of the Software, in connection with generating benchmark measurements, which is the Licensed Use precisely. The manifest line `pack /usr/bin/coremark` at `distrib/rp2040/mi.rp2040:169` may stand.

The listing may say that the board runs CoreMark. Describing a product as running an unmodified copy of the benchmark, so that the purchaser can generate benchmark data, falls within the Licensed Use. It is also, independently, nominative use of the mark to describe the thing itself, which is protected under *New Kids on the Block v. News America Publishing, Inc.*, 971 F.2d 302, 308 (9th Cir. 1992), and *Toyota Motor Sales, U.S.A., Inc. v. Tabari*, 610 F.3d 1171, 1175-76 (9th Cir. 2010). The client thus has two independent bases -- license and nominative fair use -- and the second survives even if the first is later revoked. That redundancy is worth understanding, and Section 5.5 relies on it.

**Three notice obligations attach.**

*First, Apache section 4(a): a copy of the License.* As established in Section 3.2.12, this is the live gap. The Apache 2.0 text must accompany the object-code distribution in both channels. Ship it as a release asset and make it reachable from the card's URL.

*Second, the AUA clause 2.1 ownership notice.* Clause 2.1 provides that "Licensee shall display appropriate notices regarding ownership of the Trademark in connection with the Licensed Use." This is a condition of the trademark license, and clause 2.2 makes the consequence of failure explicit: if the Licensor determines and notifies the Licensee that any act "fails to provide for proper trademark notices," the license "shall terminate." The notice must appear wherever the mark is used in connection with the Licensed Use -- the listing, the release page, and `NOTICE`.

*Third, the registered-mark notice.* Use the registration symbol on first prominent use and identify the owner and registration number. The wording recommended is:

> CoreMark(R) is a registered trademark of the Embedded Microprocessor Benchmark Consortium (EEMBC), U.S. Reg. No. 4,179,307. The CoreMark software is used here unmodified under the Apache License, Version 2.0, and the CoreMark name is used under EEMBC's CoreMark Acceptable Use Agreement. This product is not certified, endorsed, or verified by EEMBC.

That paragraph discharges clause 2.1, gives statutory notice of registration under 15 U.S.C. section 1111, and disclaims the endorsement that Apache section 6 and AUA clause 2.2 both make it important to disclaim. `NOTICE` section 13 should be amended to include it; presently section 13 describes the AUA's existence without displaying an ownership notice.

### 5.3 (ii) May benchmark results be published, and on what conditions?

**Yes, but the form of the current output makes this the sharpest issue in Analysis C, and it is not a modification issue.**

The Licensed Use is defined as "the generation of data regarding measurement and analysis of computer and embedded system benchmarking via the Software." Publishing a score in a Tindie listing is squarely within that definition: the client generates data by running the Software and displays the mark in connection with that data. The AUA therefore permits it. EEMBC's published run rules further condition the *reporting* of CoreMark results on disclosure of the compiler version and the compilation flags used, on the ground that a CoreMark number is meaningless without them. Those rules are conditions on the reporting practice rather than terms of the AUA, but a report that violates them risks being "inaccurate" within clause 2.2, which is a stated ground of termination.

**The specific problem in this tree.** Commit `09a9f496` added to `portable_fini()` in the porting layer a block that prints two lines:

```
Iterations/Sec   : <value> (microsecond-accurate)
CoreMark 1.0     : <value> / <compiler> / <memory location> (integer report)
```

The engineering reason is sound and documented. With `HAS_FLOAT=0` -- necessary because the floating-point `printf` path overflows the 144 KB process window -- `core_main.c` divides the iteration count by a whole-second `time_in_secs()`, so its own `Iterations/Sec` truncates badly (2000 divided by 8 yields 250 for an 8.254-second run that actually achieved 242.3 per second), and its own score line, guarded by `HAS_FLOAT`, does not run. The port recomputes at microsecond resolution in fixed point and prints both lines itself.

It is important to be precise about what the port's line does and does not supply, because the point bears on the recommendation. The synthesized line is emitted in the canonical field order -- score, then `COMPILER_VERSION`, then `MEM_LOCATION` -- so the compiler identification that EEMBC's run rules require is present in the program's own output rather than absent from it. What the line does not carry is the compilation flags, the iteration count, or the run duration, none of which `COMPILER_VERSION` encodes.

The legal consequence is twofold, and the two parts must be kept apart.

*The Software is still unmodified.* Nothing in the protected files changed. The AUA's clause 1.2 condition is satisfied and the trademark license stands. A contrary reading -- that computing the score in the porting layer is a modification of the Software -- is wrong, because the porting layer is the vendor's own designated fill-in file.

*But the score is not a canonical CoreMark report.* Two things make it non-canonical, and neither is the absence of compiler information. First, the figure is computed in `portable_fini()` in the porting layer rather than through `core_main.c`'s own report path, so it is the client's arithmetic rather than the benchmark's. Second, the `HAS_FLOAT=0` configuration changes the presentation to fixed point, and it is the configuration in which upstream's own reporting logic declines to emit a score at all. Publishing the resulting number as a bare "CoreMark 1.0" score -- in a listing, unqualified -- represents to the reader that it is a standard, comparable CoreMark 1.0 result. It is not, or at least it has not been shown to be, and the representation is the exposure. It is exposure under AUA clause 2.2 ("inaccurate"), and independently under section 5 of the FTC Act and California Business and Professions Code sections 17200 and 17500, as a potentially misleading performance claim in a commercial listing.

Note also that the program's *output* displays the mark, since the synthesized line contains the words "CoreMark 1.0." That display is within the Licensed Use only for so long as the Software is unmodified and the license is in force, which reinforces the standing constraint of Section 5.1.

**Recommendation, and a gate that applies before any of it.** No CoreMark score may be published until a run exceeding ten seconds has been recorded on the board. EEMBC's run rules are explicit, and the upstream `README.md` states the floor without qualification: "Results are only valid for reporting if the benchmark ran for at least 10 secs!" The only run time anywhere in this record is the 8.254 seconds referenced in commit `09a9f496`, which is below the floor, and the import commit `f5a366b1` states that the on-device score was still pending. On the present record the client has no publishable number. This is the one item in Analysis C that blocks an action the client might otherwise take immediately, and it is carried into the Section 10.1 checklist as a gate rather than as advice.

Once a conforming run exists: either omit the score from the listing entirely -- the cleanest course, and the client loses little, since the board's appeal is not its benchmark number -- or publish it with full qualification. If published, the required form is:

> CoreMark(R) result: <score> iterations/sec, integer-mode run.
>
> Compiler: `arm-none-eabi-gcc <exact version>` (as reported by the program's own result line). Flags: `<exact flags>`. Iterations: `<N>`. Run time: `<seconds>` s, exceeding EEMBC's ten-second reporting floor. Configuration: `HAS_FLOAT=0`, `MEM_METHOD=MEM_STACK`, single context.
>
> This figure is computed by the port's `core_portme.c` at microsecond resolution because the integer-mode build suppresses the benchmark's own floating-point score line. It is not a certified or EEMBC-verified CoreMark 1.0 result and should not be compared directly with certified scores.
>
> CoreMark(R) is a registered trademark of EEMBC, U.S. Reg. No. 4,179,307.

### 5.4 (iii) If the client ever renames the program

The client's instruction is that the benchmark will not be modified, so this subsection is contingency analysis only.

**The governing clause.** AUA clause 1.2 is one sentence: "Licensee shall not use the Trademark in connection with any use of a modified, derivative, or otherwise altered copy of the Software." It bars the mark's use in connection with a modified copy. It does not bar modification -- Apache 2.0 permits that freely and irrevocably -- and it does not bar the client from calling a modified copy something else.

**May the program be renamed to "testmark" or similar?** Yes. Nothing in either instrument requires that the program retain the name. Apache 2.0 imposes no naming condition, and the AUA grants a right to use the mark rather than an obligation to. A renamed program is simply a program that does not use the mark.

On the choice of name: "testmark" is a poor recommendation. It is phonetically and structurally close to COREMARK, sharing the distinctive "-mark" suffix in the same benchmarking field for the same class of goods, and a mark that near in sound, appearance, and commercial impression invites a likelihood-of-confusion analysis under the *AMF Inc. v. Sleekcraft Boats*, 599 F.2d 341 (9th Cir. 1979), factors that the client has no reason to invite. The recommended name is one that shares nothing with the mark: **`cmbench`** or, better, a name descriptive of the port rather than of the benchmark family, such as **`intbench`**. The point of renaming is distance, and a name is free.

**May a renamed *unmodified* copy still say "based on CoreMark"?** Yes, and on two grounds.

First, under the license: an unmodified copy does not engage clause 1.2 at all, so the AUA grant remains available and the statement is within the Licensed Use.

Second, and more robustly, under nominative fair use, which does not depend on the AUA. The Ninth Circuit's test, articulated in *New Kids*, 971 F.2d at 308, and refined in *Tabari*, 610 F.3d at 1175-76, has three elements: the product or service in question must be one not readily identifiable without use of the mark; only so much of the mark may be used as is reasonably necessary to identify it; and the user must do nothing that would, in conjunction with the mark, suggest sponsorship or endorsement by the holder. A statement that a program is "based on the CoreMark benchmark, unmodified" satisfies all three. There is no way to identify which benchmark the program implements without naming it; the word mark alone, without EEMBC's logo or trade dress, is the minimum necessary; and an accompanying disclaimer negates sponsorship. *Tabari* is directly instructive at 1176 in holding that the defendant may use the mark to describe the genuine goods it deals in, and that the trademark holder may not use trademark law to prevent truthful description.

**May a renamed *modified* copy reference CoreMark at all?** Here the two grounds separate, and the answer must too.

*Under the AUA: no.* Clause 1.2 is unambiguous. Any use of the Trademark in connection with a modified copy is outside the grant. Referencing the benchmark in the program's documentation or output is such a use. Contractually, the client would be in breach, and clause 2.2 would terminate the license.

*Under trademark law standing alone: a narrow truthful reference is probably still lawful,* because nominative fair use is a doctrine of trademark law, not a permission the mark-holder grants, and a mark-holder cannot by private contract enlarge its trademark rights against the world. A factually accurate statement that a program is "derived from EEMBC's CoreMark benchmark and modified; results are not comparable to CoreMark results and this is not a CoreMark benchmark" is the kind of truthful, non-confusing, comparative-origin statement that *New Kids* and *Tabari* protect.

*But the client would be in breach of contract regardless,* having accepted the AUA by exercising the trademark license, and the AUA carries an attorney's-fees clause at 4.6, a broad indemnity at 3.1, and an equitable-relief clause at 4.4 under which EEMBC may seek an injunction "without proving that any monetary damages have been sustained, and without the requirement of posting of a bond." The theoretical availability of a fair-use defense to a trademark claim is cold comfort against a contract claim with fee-shifting.

**Conclusion and recommended wording for the contingency.** If the client ever modifies the benchmark, the correct course is to rename the program and remove every reference to CoreMark from the product, the listing, and the release, retaining only the Apache attribution that Apache 2.0 section 4 requires -- which can be discharged without the mark, since it requires reproducing copyright and attribution notices from the source, not the use of the trademark in promotional text. The accompanying notice would read:

> `intbench` is derived from software licensed under the Apache License, Version 2.0, Copyright 2018 Embedded Microprocessor Benchmark Consortium. The source files have been modified from their original form. A copy of the Apache License, Version 2.0 is included with this distribution. This program is not the CoreMark benchmark, is not associated with or endorsed by EEMBC, and its results are not CoreMark results and are not comparable to them.

That paragraph names the licensor as Apache section 4 requires and as Apache section 6 permits ("reasonable and customary use in describing the origin of the Work"), discloses modification as section 4(b) requires, and uses the word CoreMark only in the negating sentence -- which is the safest possible posture, since it can confuse no one about origin.

### 5.5 (iv) Revocation risk under clause 1.4, and mitigation

**The risk.** Clause 1.4 provides that the Agreement "is perpetual unless terminated by either of the parties" and that "Licensor may terminate this Agreement effective immediately upon notice of breach." Clause 2.2 adds an independent termination trigger: if the Licensor determines and notifies the Licensee that any act in connection with the Licensed Use is inaccurate, unlawful, or offensive to good taste, fails to provide proper trademark notices, or otherwise violates the Licensee's obligations, "the license granted under this Agreement shall terminate."

The consequence on termination, in clause 1.4, is severe on its face: "Upon termination, Licensee shall immediately remove all implementations of the Trademark from the Licensed Use, and delete all digitals files and records of all materials related to the Trademark."

Applied to already-shipped product, that obligation is problematic. Boards in buyers' hands carry a program named `coremark` in flash. The client cannot recall them and cannot reach into them. A literal reading of clause 1.4 would put the client in continuing breach of an obligation it cannot perform.

**Why the practical risk is nonetheless modest, and where it is not.** Three points.

Termination is triggered by notice of breach or by a clause 2.2 determination, not at will. A client that ships an unmodified benchmark with proper notices and accurate reporting has given no ground for either. EEMBC's evident purpose in the AUA is to prevent the mark being attached to doctored benchmarks and misleading scores; a compliant hobbyist board is not the target.

The obligation on termination runs to the Licensee's own acts -- removing the mark from the Licensed Use and deleting its files -- and a court applying California law under clause 4.5 would be unlikely to read it as requiring the recall of goods already sold, an outcome the clause does not mention and that would be commercially absurd. The client should nonetheless expect to have to pull the listing, remove the program from future images, and stop publishing scores.

And nominative fair use survives termination as to truthful description, for the reasons in Section 5.4. Even with no license, the client could say that a previously shipped board contains the CoreMark benchmark, because that is a true statement about genuine goods. What the client could not do is continue to use the mark as a selling point.

**The single greatest actual risk is the inaccurate-score exposure of Section 5.3,** not modification. The client's benchmark is clean; its reporting is not yet. Fixing the reporting removes most of the clause 2.2 surface.

**Mitigation, in order of cost.**

*First and cheapest: fix the score presentation now,* per Section 5.3. This removes the clause 2.2 "inaccurate" trigger, which is the likeliest path to termination.

*Second: display the ownership notice everywhere the mark appears,* per Section 5.2. This removes the clause 2.2 "fails to provide for proper trademark notices" trigger, which is the second likeliest.

*Third: keep the benchmark unmodified,* which is already the client's instruction, and make it a standing engineering rule with a check. The tree already has an integrity mechanism in `coremark.md5` and the `check` target; the client should run it in continuous integration so that an inadvertent edit to a protected file fails the build rather than shipping.

*Fourth: preserve the exit.* The program can be omitted from the product image by deleting one line -- `pack /usr/bin/coremark` at `distrib/rp2040/mi.rp2040:169`. The sources remain in `benchmarks/coremark` in the repository, where their presence is distribution of Apache-licensed source with no trademark use in a commercial context and therefore raises no AUA issue at all. The client should treat this as a live option rather than a theoretical one. The benchmark contributes roughly 20 KB to a 979 KB root and contributes nothing to the product's appeal; the entire apparatus of Section 5 -- an Apache license text to ship, an ownership notice to display, a score to qualify, a revocable license to maintain -- exists to support a program most buyers will run once.

*Recommendation on that option.* Ship the board without `coremark`, and keep the benchmark in the repository and in the development image. This eliminates the AUA from the product entirely, eliminates the Apache section 4(a) obligation from the board channel, eliminates the score-reporting exposure from the listing, and costs the client one line and 20 KB. If the client wants the benchmark in the product, everything in Section 5 applies and is manageable -- but the cheaper answer is available and should be considered first.

**A housekeeping recommendation on `coremark.md5`.** Although the file's contents are verified to match upstream, the tree currently ships a `coremark.md5` whose `coremark.h` line differs from the one EEMBC publishes. A third party who compared the tree's integrity manifest against upstream's -- exactly the check a rights-holder or a skeptical reviewer would run -- would see a difference in the file that exists to prove the sources are untouched. That is an avoidable appearance. The better arrangement is to restore upstream's `coremark.md5` byte-for-byte, record the corrected hashes in a separate file that the local `check` target consumes, and document the upstream staleness in a comment. Prudent, not required; the client's substantive position is sound either way. This memorandum does not make that change.

## 6. Analysis D: trademarks in the listing

Every trademark use contemplated here is nominative -- the client uses others' marks to refer to the things those marks denote, not to brand its own goods. The Ninth Circuit's framework governs. Under *New Kids on the Block v. News America Publishing, Inc.*, 971 F.2d 302, 308 (9th Cir. 1992), nominative use is permitted where the product is not readily identifiable without the mark, only so much of the mark is used as is reasonably necessary, and nothing is done that would suggest sponsorship or endorsement. *Toyota Motor Sales, U.S.A., Inc. v. Tabari*, 610 F.3d 1171 (9th Cir. 2010), applies the test to a commercial seller dealing in genuine goods and holds, at 1176, that a seller may truthfully use a mark to describe the genuine products it sells, while cautioning that the manner of use matters -- prominence, logos, and domain placement can tip a permissible reference into an implied affiliation.

### 6.1 Raspberry Pi and Pico

**The strongest fact is that the boards are genuine.** The client buys authentic Raspberry Pi Picos and resells them with software added. Under the first-sale doctrine, codified for copyright at 17 U.S.C. section 109 and applied in trademark law as the doctrine of exhaustion, the resale of genuine trademarked goods is not infringement, and the reseller may use the mark to identify what it is selling. The qualification, well established in the case law, is that a reseller who materially alters the goods loses the protection, because the goods are then no longer those the mark identifies.

Does flashing firmware to a Pico materially alter it? Almost certainly not in the sense that matters. The RP2040's flash is user-writable by design; Raspberry Pi ships the board precisely so that purchasers will write firmware to it; and the alteration is fully reversible by the buyer in under a minute using the BOOTSEL procedure the README documents. A buyer who wants a blank Pico has one. The hardware is untouched. The client should nonetheless disclose the alteration plainly in the listing -- the board arrives with software installed -- both because it is true and because non-disclosure of an alteration is what converts an exhaustion defense into a claim.

**Raspberry Pi's trademark rules,** published at raspberrypi.com/trademark-rules, permit referential use of the word marks to describe compatibility and to identify genuine products, and require a license for use of the logo and for use of the marks in a product name or in a manner suggesting endorsement. The rules are the mark-holder's own statement of what it will tolerate, and following them is both good practice and evidence of good faith.

**Recommendations.**

*Permitted and recommended.* Describe the product as running on a Raspberry Pi Pico: "Runs on a genuine Raspberry Pi Pico (RP2040)." State that the board sold is a genuine Raspberry Pi Pico. Use the word marks in body text in ordinary type.

*Not permitted without a license, or not recommended.* Do not put "Raspberry Pi" or "Pico" in the product name or title. The title should name the software and the form factor without the marks -- for example, "DiscoBSD/rp2040 -- 2.11BSD UNIX-like system on a pre-flashed RP2040 board." Do not use the Raspberry Pi logo or the raspberry device. Product photographs will show the board with its own silkscreen, which is unavoidable and unobjectionable -- that is the genuine article -- but the logo should not be lifted into listing graphics, banners, or the seller's storefront branding. Do not use the marks in a seller name, shop name, or URL.

*Required disclaimer.* Place conspicuously in the listing: "Raspberry Pi and Pico are trademarks of Raspberry Pi Ltd. This product is a genuine Raspberry Pi Pico resold with additional software installed. It is not manufactured, sponsored, endorsed by, or affiliated with Raspberry Pi Ltd." `NOTICE` section 4 already carries a version of this; harmonize the two.

*A separate copyright point.* The second-stage boot code in `sys/arch/rp2040/boot2` is Raspberry Pi (Trading) Ltd.'s BSD-3-Clause code, and its third clause is a no-endorsement clause covering the same subject from the copyright side. The disclaimer above satisfies it comfortably.

### 6.2 UNIX

UNIX is a registered trademark of The Open Group, which licenses it only for systems that pass the Single UNIX Specification conformance tests. DiscoBSD/rp2040 is not certified and could not be. Calling the product "a UNIX" in the trademark sense would be a false designation actionable under Lanham Act section 43(a), 15 U.S.C. section 1125(a), and The Open Group has historically been active in policing the mark.

Nominative use to describe lineage is a different matter and is permitted. The system genuinely descends from 2.11BSD, which descends from Seventh Edition UNIX. Saying so is truthful and is the only way to say it.

**Recommendations.** Describe the system as "2.11BSD-derived" and "UNIX-like." Acceptable listing language: "a 2.11BSD-derived, UNIX-like operating system." Avoid "a UNIX system," "UNIX for the Pico," and "UNIX" standing alone as a product descriptor. Do not put UNIX in the product name. The README's existing framing -- "2.11BSD UNIX on the Raspberry Pi Pico" -- is borderline: it reads as a lineage description, which is defensible, but for a commercial listing the safer form is "2.11BSD-derived UNIX-like system." Include the notice, which `NOTICE` section 3 already carries: "UNIX is a registered trademark of The Open Group in the US and other countries."

The historical reference to Sixth Edition UNIX in the V6 emulator description is different again. That is a reference to a specific 1975 software release by its actual name, which is classic nominative use of the most protected kind. "Boots a Sixth Edition UNIX (1975) root pack on an emulated PDP-11/40" is accurate and permissible.

### 6.3 DiscoBSD and RetroBSD

Neither name appears to be a registered mark, and both function as project names. The client's use is different in kind from the others in this section: the product *is* the DiscoBSD/rp2040 port, and the client is the port's author. Using the name to identify the software is use of the client's own project name.

Two constraints nonetheless apply.

**The no-endorsement clauses.** The BSD 3-Clause license at `LICENSE` provides that neither the name of the copyright holder nor the names of its contributors may be used to endorse or promote products derived from the software without prior written permission. This is a copyright-license condition, not a trademark provision, and it binds the client. Its reach is narrow and specific: it forbids using the names *to endorse or promote*, meaning to imply that the named party backs the product. It does not forbid naming the software accurately.

**Upstream's position.** This port is a fork of Christopher Hettrick's DiscoBSD, which targets STM32 and PIC32. The listing must not suggest that the upstream project endorses, supports, or is responsible for this board. The repository name already carries the word "unofficial," which is the right instinct and should be carried into the listing.

**Recommendations.** Name the software accurately: "DiscoBSD/rp2040, an unofficial Raspberry Pi Pico port of DiscoBSD." Include: "This is an independent, unofficial port. It is not affiliated with, endorsed by, or supported by the DiscoBSD project or its maintainers, or by the RetroBSD project." Do not say "official," "certified," "supported by DiscoBSD," or anything of that shape. Do not use upstream's name in the seller's shop name. Direct support requests to the client's own issue tracker, which the README already does, and never to upstream.

### 6.4 BSD as a term

BSD is used here in two senses, and neither raises a trademark issue.

As a *lineage* term -- "2.11BSD-derived," "the BSD commands" -- it is the historical name of the Berkeley Software Distribution, which is descriptive of origin and has been in unrestricted general use for four decades. The University of California has never asserted trademark rights in it against descriptive use, and its 1999 letter went in the opposite direction, relaxing rather than tightening its requirements.

As a *license family* name -- "BSD 3-Clause," "BSD 2-Clause" -- it identifies a class of license text and is used that way universally, including by the Open Source Initiative and SPDX.

The only constraint in this area is the Berkeley no-endorsement clause: the University's name may not be used to endorse or promote the product. The listing must therefore not say anything like "Berkeley's UNIX for the Pico" or imply University sponsorship. Saying that the system descends from work done at Berkeley is a statement of fact and is fine; the courtesy acknowledgement sentence in `/etc/COPYRIGHT` and in the recommended listing block states it in the form the University itself drafted.

## 7. Analysis E: consumer and sales law for a pre-flashed board

### 7.1 The threshold point: the open-source disclaimers do not protect the seller

This is the single most important conclusion in the memorandum for the client's practical exposure, and it is counterintuitive to anyone accustomed to reading "THE SOFTWARE IS PROVIDED AS IS" in every license file.

Those disclaimers run between the upstream copyright holders and downstream licensees of the copyrighted work. They disclaim *the authors'* warranties as to *the software*. They say nothing about, and can say nothing about, the obligations that arise between this client and a person who pays this client money for a physical object shipped to their address.

The buyer's relationship with the client is a contract for the sale of goods. It is governed by Division 2 of the California Uniform Commercial Code, and, if the board is a consumer good, by the Song-Beverly Consumer Warranty Act. The client is a seller of goods, and a seller's warranty obligations arise from the sale, not from the upstream license chain. A third party's disclaimer in a file the buyer has never read cannot disclaim a warranty the client owes.

The client must not assume that pointing at `NOTICE` discharges its warranty exposure. It does not. That is why the recommended card in Section 4.7 carries the sentence distinguishing the authors' disclaimer from the seller's obligations -- a buyer who was led to believe otherwise would have been deceived, which raises Section 7.5's problems on top of the warranty problem.

### 7.2 The implied warranty of merchantability, California Commercial Code section 2314

Section 2314(1) provides that "a warranty that the goods shall be merchantable is implied in a contract for their sale if the seller is a merchant with respect to goods of that kind." A person who regularly sells pre-flashed development boards on a marketplace is a merchant with respect to those goods under section 2104(1).

Section 2314(2) sets the standard. Merchantable goods must at least pass without objection in the trade under the contract description; be fit for the ordinary purposes for which such goods are used; be adequately contained, packaged, and labeled as the agreement requires; and conform to the promises or affirmations of fact made on the container or label.

Applied here, the warranty is not onerous. The ordinary purpose of a hobbyist development board pre-flashed with an experimental operating system is to be experimented with. A board that boots to a login prompt and runs the described software is fit for that purpose. A board that is dead on arrival, or that was never flashed, is not.

Under section 2316(3)(a), an implied warranty in a non-consumer sale may be excluded by expressions like "as is" or "with all faults" which in common understanding call the buyer's attention to the exclusion and make plain there is no implied warranty; under section 2316(2), an exclusion of merchantability must mention merchantability and, if in writing, be conspicuous. That route is available for sales to businesses. It is *not* available, on its own terms, for consumer sales in California, because Song-Beverly overrides it.

### 7.3 Song-Beverly: California Civil Code sections 1791 through 1795

**Does the Act apply?** Section 1791(a) defines "consumer goods" as "any new product or part thereof that is used, bought, or leased for use primarily for personal, family, or household purposes, except for clothing and consumables." A pre-flashed hobbyist board bought by an individual to tinker with at home is bought primarily for personal purposes and is a new product. The Act very likely applies. The client should assume it does; the contrary argument -- that a development board is bought for educational or professional purposes -- is available in individual cases but cannot be the basis for a policy.

**What it requires.** Section 1792 provides that every retail sale of consumer goods in California carries an implied warranty of merchantability by the manufacturer and the retail seller, unless disclaimed in the manner the Act prescribes. Section 1791.1(a) defines merchantability in terms parallel to the Commercial Code, adding that the goods must be "fit for the ordinary purposes for which such goods are used" and "conform to the promises or affirmations of fact made on the container or label." Section 1791.1(c) fixes the duration: the implied warranty runs coextensive in duration with any express warranty, and in no event less than sixty days nor more than one year following the sale.

Critically, section 1793 provides that the Act's implied warranties may not be waived, "except as provided in Section 1792.3, 1792.4, and 1792.5." The general rule is therefore non-disclaimability, subject to one narrow escape.

**The escape: sections 1792.3 and 1792.4.** Section 1792.3 provides that there is no implied warranty of merchantability where the goods are sold on an "as is" or "with all faults" basis, but only where the sale complies with section 1792.4.

Section 1792.4(a) sets out what compliance requires. The seller must, *prior to the sale*, provide the buyer with a **conspicuous writing attached to the goods** that clearly informs the buyer, in simple and concise language, of each of three things: that the goods are being sold on an "as is" or "with all faults" basis; that the entire risk as to the quality and performance of the goods is with the buyer; and that should the goods prove defective following their purchase, the buyer and not the manufacturer, distributor, or retailer assumes the entire cost of all necessary servicing or repair.

Section 1792.4(b) supplies the sanction: if the seller fails to give the required notice, the sale is not an "as is" sale and the implied warranties apply notwithstanding the "as is" label.

**The consequence for a Tindie listing is decisive and changes the client's plan.** The statute requires a writing *attached to the goods*, given *prior to the sale*. A line of text in a web listing is neither attached to the goods nor, self-evidently, a writing attached to anything. The client cannot disclaim Song-Beverly's implied warranty through the listing alone. The shipped card is therefore a legal requirement in this channel, not a courtesy.

Two courses follow, and the client should take both.

*Attach the notice to the goods.* Include in the package, affixed to or immediately enclosing the board, a card carrying the three statutory disclosures in the statutory sense. Note the timing problem this does not solve: section 1792.4(a) requires the notice *prior to the sale*, and a card in a box shipped after purchase arrives afterward. The standard practice in mail-order and online sale is to display the identical notice conspicuously in the offer, so the buyer receives it before committing, and to repeat it attached to the goods. Doing both is the only way to give the statute its full effect at a distance. The client should do both and should not assume either alone suffices.

*Do not rely on the disclaimer working.* Given the timing difficulty, the client should price and operate as though the implied warranty of merchantability applies for the statutory minimum of sixty days, and should simply replace or refund boards that fail. At this product's price and volume, a no-questions replacement policy for dead boards is cheaper than the legal apparatus required to avoid one, and it removes essentially all of the exposure in this section. This is a commercial recommendation, but it is the right one.

**The exact statutory wording, form, and type size of the section 1792.4 notice must be confirmed by counsel against the current statute.** Section 1792.4(a) specifies the required content in three enumerated clauses and requires a conspicuous writing attached to the goods; the precise formatting requirements, including whether a minimum type size is imposed, are an item for verification and appear in Section 11.

**Two further limits.** Section 1792.3's "as is" route is unavailable where the seller gives an express warranty or enters into a service contract with the buyer -- which is a reason to give no express warranty. And nothing in Song-Beverly permits disclaiming liability for personal injury, nor does it reach fraud or misrepresentation.

### 7.4 Magnuson-Moss: give no written warranty

The federal Magnuson-Moss Warranty Act, 15 U.S.C. sections 2301 et seq., applies to written warranties on consumer products. Section 2308(a) provides that a supplier who makes a written warranty may not disclaim or modify any implied warranty to a consumer; section 2308(b) permits limiting an implied warranty's duration to that of a limited written warranty, if conscionable and conspicuously disclosed.

The operative consequence is simple. If the client gives *no* written warranty, section 2308's bar is never triggered and the state-law analysis in Section 7.3 stands alone. If the client gives even a modest written warranty -- "guaranteed to boot," "30-day warranty" -- it forfeits the ability to disclaim implied warranties under federal law entirely.

**Recommendation: give no written warranty.** Offer a replacement or refund as a matter of policy, stated as a policy rather than as a warranty, and do not use the word "warranty" or "guarantee" in the listing. A stated return policy is not a written warranty within the meaning of 15 U.S.C. section 2301(6), which defines the term as a written affirmation or promise relating to material or workmanship and affirming that the product is defect-free or will meet a specified level of performance over a specified period. Counsel should confirm the line between the two for the client's exact wording.

### 7.5 Truthfulness of the listing: FTC Act section 5, and Business and Professions Code sections 17200 and 17500

**Federal.** Section 5(a) of the FTC Act, 15 U.S.C. section 45(a), prohibits unfair or deceptive acts or practices in or affecting commerce. A representation is deceptive if it is likely to mislead a consumer acting reasonably in the circumstances and is material to a purchasing decision. Objective performance claims must be substantiated at the time they are made.

**California.** Business and Professions Code section 17500 makes it unlawful to disseminate any statement concerning goods offered for sale which is untrue or misleading and which is known, or which by the exercise of reasonable care should be known, to be untrue or misleading. Business and Professions Code section 17200 defines unfair competition to include any unlawful, unfair, or fraudulent business act or practice and any act of unfair, deceptive, untrue, or misleading advertising, and incorporates section 17500 by reference. Section 17200 has a four-year limitations period under section 17208 and, following Proposition 64, requires a private plaintiff to have suffered injury in fact and lost money or property; remedies are restitution and injunctive relief rather than damages.

The practical significance is that section 17200 gives a private plaintiff a cause of action for conduct that violates any other law -- the "unlawful" prong borrows the predicate. A violation of a Caldera license condition, a breach of the CoreMark AUA, or a false statement about certification could each be repackaged as a section 17200 claim.

**Applied to this listing, the claims to police are these.**

*The benchmark score,* per Section 5.3. An unqualified "CoreMark 1.0" figure produced through a non-standard path, published to sell a product, is an objective performance claim that must be substantiated in the form in which it is made. Qualify it or omit it.

*Certification and endorsement claims.* Do not say "UNIX certified," "official," "licensed by," "approved by," "EEMBC verified," or "endorsed." Each would be false.

*Licensing claims.* Do not say "all licenses cleared," "fully licensed," or "no legal encumbrances." Section 4 explains why a categorical statement about the Caldera chain would be inaccurate. Say what is true: every component is under a permissive license and none is GPL, which is accurate and is what buyers actually want to know.

*Specification claims.* Every number in the listing must be true and current: 264 KB of SRAM, 2 MB of flash, a 128 KB kernel, roughly 979 KB of usable root blocks, a 144 KB process window, 112 command names, 115200 baud. The README supports each. If a figure changes, the listing changes with it. Tindie's own terms independently require accurate specifications.

*Condition and provenance.* State that the board is a genuine Raspberry Pi Pico purchased new and flashed by the seller, and that the firmware can be removed by the buyer via BOOTSEL. Both are true and both forestall complaints.

*Software limitations.* State plainly what the system does not do: no networking, one process resident at a time, no memory protection programmed, no preprocessor or headers for the on-board compiler. Omitting known material limitations from a listing is itself a deceptive practice, and these are limitations a buyer would care about.

### 7.6 Statutes that do not apply, and two that were not asked about

For completeness.

**California's Automatic Renewal Law,** Business and Professions Code sections 17600 et seq., governs subscriptions and continuous-service offers with automatic renewal. A one-time sale of a board has no renewal term and is outside it entirely.

**The Consumers Legal Remedies Act,** Civil Code sections 1750 et seq., does apply to the sale of goods to consumers and prohibits a list of enumerated deceptive practices in section 1770, including misrepresenting the source, sponsorship, approval, or certification of goods, and representing that goods have characteristics they do not have. It is not a statute that requires anything affirmative of the client, but it supplies a private right of action with attorney's fees for the misrepresentations Section 7.5 already tells the client to avoid. It should be on the client's radar; it was omitted from the question and is added here.

**California's Proposition 65,** Health and Safety Code section 25249.6, requires a clear and reasonable warning before knowingly exposing a person to a listed chemical. Whether an unenclosed electronic board triggers it depends on its constituent materials and on whether the client falls within the ten-employee threshold of section 25249.11(b). The client is likely below the threshold and therefore exempt, but the question depends on facts and on the composition of goods the client does not manufacture. It is added to Section 11.

**Product-safety regulation.** A USB-powered board drawing under 100 mA at 5 V is not a regulated consumer product under the Consumer Product Safety Act in any category obviously applicable, is not a children's product, and carries no battery. FCC equipment-authorization obligations attach to the Raspberry Pi Pico as manufactured and were discharged by Raspberry Pi; reselling the genuine board unmodified does not impose new obligations on the reseller. Counsel should confirm that reflashing firmware does not disturb the existing authorization, which turns on whether the change affects the device's RF characteristics -- for a board with no radio, it does not.

### 7.7 The safety facts and the recommended statement

The board ships without an enclosure, with exposed conductors and components, powered at 5 V from a USB connection. The hazards are modest and real: static discharge can damage it, a short across the pins can damage the board or the host's USB port, and the regulator warms in use. None presents a meaningful risk of personal injury at 5 V and under 100 mA, but the client should say so rather than say nothing, both because a buyer should know and because disclosure of known characteristics is the cheapest defense against a claim that they were concealed.

## 8. Analysis F: export controls and other regulatory matters

### 8.1 The Export Administration Regulations

The firmware is published, publicly available, open-source software. Section 734.3(b)(3) of the EAR, 15 C.F.R. section 734.3(b)(3), excludes from the scope of the EAR software that is "published," and section 734.7(a) defines information as published when it "has been made available to the public without restrictions upon its further dissemination," including availability on the internet without access controls.

Publishing the UF2 files on a public GitHub release page, with the source in a public repository, is publication in exactly this sense. There is no access control, no registration, no fee, and no restriction on redistribution -- the licenses affirmatively permit it. The software is therefore **not subject to the EAR**, and no classification, license, or license exception is required for its publication or for its distribution on a board. Where a classification is nonetheless demanded by a form or a shipper, EAR99 is the correct entry for the accompanying hardware, and "not subject to the EAR, published under 15 C.F.R. 734.7" is the correct statement for the software.

Two qualifications, neither of which applies on present facts. First, encryption software receives distinct treatment under section 742.15(b) and section 740.13(e), which for publicly available encryption source code requires a notification email to BIS. This firmware contains no cryptographic functionality -- no cipher, no key exchange, no hashing for security purposes -- so the encryption rules are not engaged. The client should confirm this remains true if cryptographic code is ever added; it would convert a no-obligation situation into a notification obligation. Second, the exclusion does not apply to software subject to an access restriction, so the client should not put the releases behind a login, a paywall, or a click-through that restricts redistribution.

### 8.2 The hardware

The Raspberry Pi Pico is a mass-market microcontroller development board, manufactured and exported by Raspberry Pi Ltd. under whatever classification applies to it -- in practice EAR99 or an equivalent mass-market treatment for a general-purpose Cortex-M0+ board with no controlled functionality. The client is reselling genuine goods and is not the manufacturer or the original exporter.

The client's residual obligations are the ordinary ones that attach to any exporter of low-technology goods, and they are screening obligations rather than licensing ones: do not ship to embargoed destinations, and do not ship to a party on the Specially Designated Nationals list maintained by OFAC or on the BIS Denied Persons, Entity, or Unverified lists. Tindie's terms make sanctions and export-control compliance the seller's responsibility explicitly, so this is a marketplace obligation as well as a legal one. For a domestic-focused hobbyist seller, this is discharged by declining orders from embargoed destinations and by spot-checking unusual international orders against the consolidated screening list at trade.gov. The current list of embargoed destinations should be checked at the time of shipment rather than taken from this memorandum, since it changes.

### 8.3 Nothing else applies

No other federal regulatory regime attaches on these facts. The product is not a medical device, not a motor-vehicle part, not a radio transmitter, not a device subject to Department of Energy efficiency standards, and not subject to the Digital Millennium Copyright Act's anticircumvention provisions in any respect -- nothing here circumvents any technological protection measure, and the board's flash is user-writable by design.

State-level obligations are the ordinary ones of any small seller: a California seller's permit and sales-tax collection on California sales, and marketplace-facilitator arrangements for tax on Tindie sales, which the marketplace typically handles. Those are outside the scope of this memorandum and are flagged in Section 11.

## 9. Analysis G: the Tindie marketplace contract

The Tindie Terms of Service, effective June 30, 2026, operated by EETREE LLC, a Washington limited liability company, add a contractual layer on top of everything above. They do not change what the licenses require. They change who else can complain when the client falls short, and what it costs.

### 9.1 The operative clauses

**Section 6, first representation:** "Sellers must own or be authorized to sell each product and must provide accurate photos, descriptions, specifications, prices, stock levels, processing times, and shipping information."

**Section 6, allocation of responsibility:** "Sellers are responsible for product safety, labeling, warranties they offer, taxes, customs declarations, sanctions, export controls, consumer laws, intellectual-property rights, and all other legal obligations applicable to their products and business."

**Section 7:** "Tindie may prohibit products that are illegal, unsafe, counterfeit, infringing, deceptive, regulated, sanctioned, or inconsistent with the marketplace's purpose."

**Section 13:** "You represent that you have the necessary rights to submit the content and that it does not infringe another party's rights. Tindie may remove content or listings in response to credible infringement or policy reports."

**Section 18:** "You agree to indemnify and hold harmless EETREE LLC and its affiliates from claims, losses, liabilities, and reasonable expenses arising from your products, listings, conduct, legal violations, infringement, taxes, or breach of these Terms."

**Section 19** requires informal resolution before formal proceedings and names no governing law or forum.

The Terms contain no clause addressing third-party software licenses specifically, and no exclusivity provision. The client remains free to publish the same firmware on GitHub, to sell elsewhere, and to distribute the software however the licenses permit.

### 9.2 What they add

**The "authorized to sell" representation is the one that engages Section 4.** By listing, the client represents that it is authorized to sell each product. The product bundles software distributed under the Caldera grant, whose validity Section 4 concludes is doubtful as to title even though the practical risk is low. The client's representation is nonetheless defensible: it relies on a facially valid written license from the entity that operated the UNIX business, on the universal practice of the field, and on the absence of any contrary assertion by any title-holder in twenty-four years. Authorization does not mean certainty, and no seller of anything warrants against every latent title defect. But the client should understand that it is making this representation, should not embellish it in the listing, and should keep the Caldera analysis on file.

**The "accurate descriptions" representation converts Section 7.5 into a contract term.** Every specification in the listing, every claim about what the board runs, and any benchmark score are now warranted to Tindie as well as regulated by the FTC Act and sections 17200 and 17500. The CoreMark score issue of Section 5.3 is accordingly a three-front exposure: AUA clause 2.2, consumer-protection law, and section 6 of the Terms.

**The section 18 indemnity is the most significant addition.** It is broad -- claims, losses, liabilities, and reasonable expenses -- and it reaches "your products, listings, conduct, legal violations, infringement, taxes, or breach of these Terms." The practical effect is that if a rights-holder complains to Tindie about the client's listing, the client bears not only its own exposure but Tindie's costs in responding. The client should note that this indemnity operates in parallel with the CoreMark AUA's clause 3.1 indemnity running to EEMBC, so a single CoreMark misstep could trigger two indemnities. That is a further argument for the Section 5.5 recommendation to omit the benchmark from the product image.

**Sections 7 and 13 mean the listing can disappear on an unadjudicated complaint.** Both give Tindie discretion to remove listings in response to infringement or policy reports, and neither requires an adjudication or conditions removal on the report's merit. The client's exposure here is commercial rather than legal: a complaint from Raspberry Pi Ltd., The Open Group, EEMBC, or a UNIX title-holder could take the listing down before the client had any chance to answer. This is a strong practical reason to get the notices and disclaimers right before launch rather than after a complaint, and to keep a compliance file -- the license texts, this memorandum, the Caldera letter, the CoreMark verification -- ready to produce on short notice.

**Section 6's allocation confirms Section 7's conclusion from a second direction.** The Terms state expressly that the seller, not Tindie, is responsible for product safety, warranties, and consumer laws. The client therefore cannot look to the marketplace to absorb any part of the Song-Beverly or Commercial Code exposure analyzed in Section 7. Tindie is a venue, not a co-seller, and the buyer's contract counterparty is the client. Whether Tindie acts as a marketplace facilitator for sales-tax purposes is a separate question with a possibly different answer, and is flagged in Section 11.

**Section 19's silence on governing law is a minor open point.** Without a choice-of-law or forum clause, a dispute between the client and EETREE LLC would be resolved under ordinary conflicts principles, likely pointing toward Washington as the operator's domicile. The informal-resolution requirement is a procedural step, not a bar. Neither materially affects the client's planning.

### 9.3 Conclusion on Analysis G

The Terms impose no new substantive compliance obligation. Everything they require -- accurate descriptions, authorization to sell, responsibility for intellectual property and consumer law -- the client must do anyway. What they add is a second aggrieved party with an indemnity, and a discretionary takedown power exercisable without adjudication. Both counsel the same thing: get the listing right before it goes up.
